\section{Blockchain-Assisted Assurance: Selection and a Specified Pattern}

\subsection{The Decision to Use a Ledger}\label{sec:ledger-choice}

Ledger adoption begins with writers, state, and authority. W\"ust and Gervais organize the decision around the need to store shared state, the number and trust relationships of writers, and the availability of a trusted party \citep{wust2018need}. Locher et al. examine which functions of a trusted third party can be replaced by distributed agreement \citep{locher2018when}. For mission assurance, the relevant requirement is that no single organization may silently change accepted membership, policy, or revocation history, while authorized parties retain a mutually verifiable state.

In the coalition example, A alone must not replace a sensor-acceptance policy, C must not remove a previously acknowledged batch after a gateway incident, and D must not declare its own data accepted by the coalition. Four independent organizations can jointly validate those transitions. A permissioned ledger is one implementation of this requirement. Its value is confined to such shared-governance conditions and the operational services supplied by the chosen implementation.

The strongest alternative is a transparency service whose checkpoints and state transitions are independently witnessed and checked under the same authorization rules. Such a service can satisfy the same safety requirements. A consistency-only witness detects or exposes inconsistent histories but does not by that act establish that a policy change was authorized. Adding joint policy validation closes that gap. The replay in Section~\ref{sec:trace} consequently finds equivalent decisions for the ledger and policy-validating log. Architecture selection then turns on implementation complexity, privacy, availability, recovery, and measured cost. No intrinsic ledger superiority is inferred.

Governance viability is also an adoption condition. Maersk and IBM discontinued TradeLens after it failed to achieve the required global industry collaboration and commercial viability, despite a technically viable platform \citep{maersk2022tradelens}. This maritime case motivates evaluating incentives, participation, exit, and operating responsibility. It does not establish a failure of a particular consensus protocol.

\subsection{Mission Requirements and Consensus Candidates}\label{sec:consensus}

A requirement-based selection process, as advocated by \citet{svarcmajer2026consensus}, specifies the fault model before choosing a protocol. Here the coalition design assumes at most one Byzantine administrative domain among four independently controlled shore nodes. Safety must hold during a USV partition; new global decisions may stop when a responsive quorum is unavailable. The illustrative reconciliation deadline is 60 s after an event becomes available for shore processing. Membership is stable during a mission and changes through a jointly approved epoch transition. Shore nodes have adequate compute and power; UUVs do not participate in consensus. Consensus traffic uses shore/radio infrastructure, with only compact evidence commitments crossing the acoustic link. These are design requirements, rather than measurements of a deployed mission.

Table~\ref{tab:consensus} compares representative families. Normal-path message complexity is a selection indicator, conditional on batching, signature aggregation, dissemination, and view changes. Fault tolerance and liveness assumptions remain decisive. In particular, a DAG organization does not remove the need to state which agreement protocol orders or certifies its data.

Raft and Byzantine protocols solve different fault problems \citep{raft2014,pbft1999,hotstuff2019}. CometBFT uses its own round-based consensus and gossip rules \citep{cometbft2026}; Narwhal is a dissemination/mempool component, while Tusk supplies an associated ordering protocol \citep{narwhal2022}. Authority-based UAV acquisition, as implemented in BUS, illustrates placement on capable validators \citep{islam2019bus}. Lightweight IoT proposals similarly motivate moving consensus away from constrained endpoints \citep{dorri2017towards}; the optimization must be assessed together with the resulting administrative dependencies.

Fault assumptions are stated separately for infrastructure and applications. A Byzantine ordering service concerns agreement despite arbitrary behavior of replicas; it does not prevent an authorized producer from supplying a false observation. Endorsement constrains who approves a proposed state update, while the chaincode defines the deterministic rule being approved. A contract can consistently implement an unsuitable rule, so policy approval also requires evidence about its mission consequence. The application must specify whether emergency authority can bypass ordinary endorsement and how that exception is recorded and reviewed. Without an explicit exception, loss of a mandatory signer is an intentional loss of fresh global authorization.

Partition tolerance likewise has two meanings that must be separated. Safety preserves a consistent accepted history when messages are delayed or unavailable. Availability concerns whether a particular request can obtain the needed ordering and application approvals before its deadline. A shore quorum may remain healthy while the USV is disconnected, allowing shore revocations to progress while a UUV continues under cached authority. If the shore quorum itself is divided, new global updates may stop even after the gateway returns. Evaluations should vary these two partition locations independently. Classical consistency/availability results motivate this distinction under network partition \citep{gilbert2002brewer}; they do not specify the vehicle's local safety policy.

Three scenario decisions follow. A single-agency inspection mission can use an authenticated database or witnessed log, adding Raft replication for crash availability. The four-organization response mission requires Byzantine ordering plus joint application authorization, or an equivalent validating log; current Fabric SmartBFT is a concrete candidate \citep{fabric2026bft}. A disconnected UUV group uses signed local records, store-and-forward transport, and bounded delegation, with no requirement for acoustic consensus. Increasing validator count only helps when it increases independent fault domains while preserving a reachable quorum. Actual latency and energy must be measured for the selected deployment.

\subsection{A Coalition Provenance-and-Revocation Pattern}\label{sec:pattern}

Five recurring ledger roles are membership/authorization, evidence commitments, policy/model version coordination, accountable audit, and post-partition reconciliation. The specified pattern combines commitments and reconciliation; Table~\ref{tab:blockchain-patterns} places the other roles around it. The application logic is specified in the supplementary pseudocode, and its temporal and authorization rules are exercised by the replay.

\textbf{Placement and endorsement.} Each organization A--D hosts an endorsing peer and a separate ordering node on capable shore infrastructure. The illustrative BFT service has four orderers, tolerating one Byzantine node under its protocol assumptions. Administrative separation, rather than merely four processes, supplies the independence. The application endorsement rule is \texttt{AND(A, B, OR(C, D))}; channel configuration and chaincode-definition approval require the same coalition authority. A and B are mandatory, so application progress can stop even while three orderers remain responsive. The USV forwards and caches signed evidence, with no consensus vote. A partitioned gateway buffers batches and retries idempotently after reconnection. UUV safety control uses its local envelope throughout.

\textbf{State and encoding.} The state keys are mission/policy/epoch, producer/key/history, delegation/identifier, batch/producer/sequence, and judgment/record/policy. A compact source record has a 154-byte fixed-width body: version (1), kind (1), mission ID (16), producer ID (16), sequence (4), event lower and upper bounds (8 each), authority epoch (4), policy hash (32), payload hash (32), and parent/root commitment (32). Integers use network byte order; time is signed seconds in the declared mission epoch. A 64-byte Ed25519 signature gives a 218-byte record. Domain-separated signing and hashing bind record type and version. A batch uses the same body layout with a Merkle root and previous-batch root, plus a two-byte record count: 156 bytes before signature and 220 bytes signed. Ten signed records are retained off chain. The released canonical JSON example submitted by a gateway is exactly 525 application bytes, excluding the file's trailing newline.

\textbf{Deterministic chaincode operations.} \texttt{CommitBatch} reads the mission epoch, gateway role, and last batch sequence; verifies the compact gateway signature and policy binding; rejects an inconsistent sequence or duplicate identifier with different content; and records the root. An identical retry returns the existing result. This commits an auditable batch, not a claim that every leaf is fit for use. \texttt{JudgeRecord} verifies a supplied source signature and inclusion proof, reads the producer's historical key status and delegation, and writes a separate eligible/revoked/quarantined judgment using the interval rule in Section~\ref{sec:trace}. \texttt{UpdatePolicy} and \texttt{RevokeKey} require the coalition endorsement rule and monotone epochs; revocation records preserve their effective time. All state read for a decision enters the Fabric read set, allowing validation to reject stale concurrent updates. Chaincode obtains no external clock or oracle value during execution; authorized submitted evidence supplies those inputs.

\textbf{Commit status and constrained verification.} Fabric ordering precedes peer validation, so block inclusion is not proof of a valid world-state update \citep{androulaki2018hyperledger,hyperledger2026fabric}. After observing a valid commit, three distinct organizations satisfying A+B+(C or D) sign an application receipt containing the mission, policy/authority epoch, batch root, sequence, event span, and count. Receipt-signing code must check the commit validation status and the expected application state. A UUV checks authorized signer identities against its pinned epoch, signatures, monotone checkpoint sequence, source-record signature, and the Merkle branch. For ten leaves padded to 16, the branch has four 32-byte hashes. These checks establish a jointly attested application checkpoint and inclusion under pinned keys; they do not constitute native verification of all Fabric consensus and peer-validation execution. A new epoch requires an authenticated transition from the pinned epoch. The receipt is an additional application trust boundary that must be implemented and tested.

\subsection{Communication Budget and Partition Sensitivity}\label{sec:budget}

The receipt and retrieval paths serve different evidence needs. A vehicle receiving a checkpoint can verify that independent organizations acknowledged the committed root under a pinned authority epoch. To appraise an observation, it still obtains the relevant leaf, its inclusion path, and any required source or transformation evidence. A commitment-only contact therefore supports a narrower claim than complete evidence delivery. Retrieval policy can prioritize recent high-consequence products and defer bulk imagery until a higher-bandwidth contact, while recording which required inputs remain unavailable. A failed retrieval is represented as missing evidence rather than a successful integrity check.

Clock and key transitions also affect the usable lifetime of a proof. A cached checkpoint remains evidence about a historical commitment after a new policy is issued, but its authority to support a new decision depends on the relying policy and authenticated transition history. A vehicle with an uncertain clock cannot establish that an expiry condition still holds by inspecting a ledger root. It needs a bounded time interval or a more restrictive fallback. Similarly, a fresh receipt cannot make an untrustworthy acquisition timestamp credible. These requirements bind the application-level proof to the same temporal assumptions used in the replay and define what a constrained verifier must do when those assumptions fail.

Ed25519 signatures contain 64 bytes \citep{rfc8032}; ML-DSA-44 signatures contain 2420 bytes \citep{fips204}. These are alternative compact application profiles, not claims that either encoding is the default Fabric transaction-signature format. Fabric enrollment certificates, endorsements, proposal responses, read/write sets, and envelopes add deployment-dependent bytes. Only the gateway transports those full transactions on the shore network. Their total size and resource cost require a configured implementation; the 525-byte JSON value is not presented as a measured Fabric transaction size.

For a message of $B$ bytes and net payload rate $R$ bit/s, the serialization lower bound is $8B/R$. Table~\ref{tab:budget} uses 100, 1000, and 5000 bit/s as scenario inputs. It excludes propagation, contact waiting, modem framing, BPSec overhead, loss, retries, and certificate/epoch updates. Those factors increase end-to-end delay and must be added for a specified modem and transport configuration.

The 697-byte downlink comprises a 218-byte source record, 128-byte branch, two-byte leaf index, 156-byte checkpoint, one-byte signer bitmap, and three 64-byte signatures. At one ten-record batch per minute, the 220-byte commitment needs 29.3 net bit/s on average, whereas sending all ten signed records needs 290.7 bit/s. Commitment-only delivery therefore fits some contacts that cannot carry full evidence, at the cost of later retrieval and incomplete immediate appraisal. Sending one ten-record ML-DSA-44 batch commitment at the same cadence requires 343.5 bit/s. Public keys are assumed provisioned; key distribution and post-quantum verification time remain separate costs.

The replay also enumerates 63 combinations of validator count (4, 7, 10), responsive count (quorum minus one, quorum, all), and partition duration (0, 10, 30, 58, 59, 60, 120 s). With assumed post-contact delays of 2 s for a ledger path and 1 s for a validating-log path, a 60 s deadline permits partitions of at most 58 and 59 s, respectively, when a quorum is reachable. Below quorum, neither completes a fresh joint decision. This sensitivity calculation exhibits a regime in which the log is faster and no ledger safety gain exists; its delays are illustrative inputs, not benchmarks. Application endorsement availability must be checked in addition to ordering quorum. The result motivates measuring both architectures under the same workload, governance, and fault domains.
