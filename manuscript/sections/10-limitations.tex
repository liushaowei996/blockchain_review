\section{Limitations of the Review and Framework}

This review uses a narrative design and a focused English-language evidence corpus with a principal 2016--2026 window. It supports conceptual synthesis across robotics, communications, cybersecurity, distributed systems, provenance, trust modeling, and governance. Source discovery, grouping, and emphasis depend partly on author judgment, and language, publication, and acquisition selection can affect the resulting picture. The evidence matrix records these choices but does not establish exhaustive coverage. Estimates of literature prevalence or pooled effectiveness would require a reproducible search and screening protocol, a defined sampling frame, and compatible study outcomes.

Full-text availability, screening, and analytical coding are distinct stages. Sixteen sources received claim-level coding for the detailed comparisons in this version; the broader inventory includes records with more limited inspection. Bibliographic verification establishes source identity, not the validity or applicability of its findings. The selective coding supports bounded examples rather than a complete distribution of methods, evidence maturity, or limitations. Independent duplicate coding and an inter-rater agreement estimate are not reported, leaving the consistency of the framework's application to be assessed in a subsequent study.

Much evidence is transferred from adjacent domains. UAV security, IoT reputation, road-vehicle trust, industrial provenance, and permissioned enterprise ledgers provide candidate mechanisms, but their threat distributions, time scales, connectivity, energy, and governance may differ from air--surface--underwater operations. This review identifies transfer assumptions conceptually; it does not measure transfer performance. Even within maritime systems, acoustic conditions and operational practices vary. These differences require target-environment validation rather than direct reuse of published performance estimates.

OAL, the dependency relation set, CTG loop, evidence profile, hypotheses, and CDUTP form the authors' conceptual architecture. This article supplies no new integrated implementation, formal composition proof, mission experiment, or demonstration of improved safety, calibration, availability, or audit performance. The four object types require evaluation for coverage and consistent application; additional application settings may motivate refinements. Practical graph implementations require rules for abstraction, correlation, privacy, evidence expiry, cycles, and conflicting claims, as well as measured computation and communication costs. The general assessment $T_o(t,c,m)$ need not be probabilistic; a probabilistic implementation additionally requires a defined event and horizon, an explicit statistical model, and calibration evaluated under relevant operating conditions.

The evidence categories organize claim scope rather than measure rigor on a single scale. The proposed research hypotheses depend on concrete comparators, loss functions, and scenario distributions, and neither the preferred architectural trade-offs nor their effect sizes follow from the taxonomy alone. CDUTP remains a profile proposal without a normative schema, interoperable implementation, or conformance test suite. Formal analysis, controlled studies, field evidence, and multi-party governance exercises are complementary steps toward assessing these contributions.

Finally, the public-evidence focus supports reproducible analysis of assurance, resilience, audit, and responsible governance in civil and publicly documented defense-support settings. Sensitive operational details remain within the governance and disclosure controls of the organizations that hold them. Future authorized studies can evaluate the framework under additional contested-environment and platform-specific conditions.
